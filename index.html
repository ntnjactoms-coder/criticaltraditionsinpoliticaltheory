01 · Orientation

Course overview

Description. This course introduces students to the critical traditions in political theory that question, revise and reconstruct the foundations of modern political thought. It engages with Marxism, feminism, postcolonialism, ecological thought and other radical approaches that interrogate power, domination and inequality. Students approach theory as a mode of critique and resistance, foregrounding the relationship between knowledge, emancipation and politics. Through a study of diverse thinkers and traditions, they develop a critical perspective on the structures of capitalism, patriarchy, colonialism and ecological crisis.

Course objectives

The course aims to:

  • Introduce students to major critical traditions in political theory
  • Help students understand theory as a mode of critique
  • Engage students with Marxism, feminism and postcolonialism

09 · Assessment brief

CIA 1: Close reading, annotating and summarising

20 marks · CO allocation: CO1 10, CO2 10.

An individual, in-class activity based on a text selected by the instructor and aligned with the syllabus. Each student receives a copy of the text with wide margins for annotation. Students read the text carefully and critically, identify key ideas and record their observations as marginal annotations. They then write a concise summary of about 300 words showing their understanding of the central argument, themes and significant details. Different students may receive different texts. Submission: in class (plagiarism policy not applicable).

Evaluation criterionMarks

10 · Longitudinal reflection

CIA 3: Longitudinal reflective assessment

20 marks · CO allocation: CO3 10, CO4 10.

Three individual written pieces of 500–800 words each, written over the semester.

Essay 1 · Beginning of semester · 5 marks

Essay 1 (beginning of the semester, 5 marks): How do you perceive the relationship between technology and human freedom? Reflect on how your social location and subjectivity (class or caste location, gender, community, political affiliation, age or life stage, broader personal and moral commitments, family background and so on) may have shaped this perspective.

Essay 2 · Towards the end of semester · 5 marks

Essay 2 (towards the end of the semester, 5 marks): How do you perceive the relationship between technology and human freedom?

Reflective essay · Towards the end of semester · 10 marks

Reflective Essay (towards the end of the semester, 10 marks): How do you assess your own intellectual development during the course? Discuss how engagement with critical traditions in political theory helped you reaffirm, revise, complicate or abandon some of your earlier assumptions. Reflect not only on changes in your conclusions but also on changes in how you think about political questions.

Note to students. These assignments reward genuine thinking rather than polished prose. Students will not be penalised for imperfect writing, uncertainty, changing their minds or making mistakes. Submissions that are wholly or substantially generated by AI will not be accepted; they will be returned and must be rewritten independently. An honest but imperfect response is valued more highly than a polished response that does not reflect the student’s own thinking.
